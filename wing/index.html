Aeroplane wing

Fly it slow, pull hard, dive fast

A wing makes lift by turning air downwards. Tilt it to the oncoming air (angle of attack) and the air over the curved top speeds up and its pressure drops, the air underneath slows and pushes up, and the whole flow leaves the trailing edge heading down. Lift goes with the speed squared, so slow down and the wing has to tilt more, until the air can't follow the top surface any more and the wing stalls. All that lift bends the wing up like a diving board: the top skin is squashed, the bottom skin is stretched, and the root carries the lot. Go too fast and a different thing can happen: the air starts pumping energy into the wing's own wobble, and that's flutter.

A toy, not a simulator. Real: lift = ½ρV²S·CL, the stall, induced drag, ice and flaps changing the stall, the flow in the inset (potential flow round a Joukowski aerofoil with the Kutta condition, so the timelines really do show the top arriving first), a wing as a cantilever with the top skin in compression and the bottom in tension, and flutter. Real rules: limit load 2.5 g (14 CFR 25.337), ultimate = 1.5 × limit (25.303), free from flutter beyond the dive speed (25.629). Real tests: the 787's ultimate load test (March 2010, 150 %, tips up about 7.6 m) and the 777-9 test wing that failed at 148 % (2019).
